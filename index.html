Current project: Learning in Zero Gravity Google Scholar
Robot learning, and vision-language-action models (VLAs) in particular, is limited by data. Simulators are cheap, but we mostly use them to recreate ordinary scenes under Earth’s physics and to train under regular task settings, so they give us far less than they could. My research is focused on coming up with imaginative ways to extract more useful experience out of simulators.

My current work explores a space–Earth symbiosis in robotics. The pattern has surfaced in many technologies before: work done for space, from the CMOS image sensors now in phone cameras to memory foam, came back to improve life on Earth. In robotics it can run both ways. Training under space-like physics such as zero gravity can give robots more diverse experience that makes them better on Earth, and robots trained on Earth are the starting point for robots that will work in space (see Space applications).

Idea 1

Zero-gravity training for better performance on Earth in progress

Train robot experts with gravity switched off until they touch the object, then distill their spatially diverse demonstrations into VLAs to improve ordinary, Earth-gravity manipulation. Project page.

More from the simulator: Change the simulator's physics: switching gravity off turns ordinary tabletop tasks into data that covers the whole 3D workspace.

The enabling mechanism: Switching gravity off is not enough on its own: a floating object drifts out of reach, and in pure zero gravity policies exploit the floating object instead of learning the skill. I built two mechanisms that make it trainable. Selective containment volumes: thin walls that block the floating object but not the robot arm (separate collision groups), are invisible to cameras, and can release when the task needs it. Gravity that switches on at contact or grasp: the object floats while the robot explores, and the task itself is finished under normal gravity. Gravity is controlled per environment inside one GPU-simulated batch, so thousands of parallel environments each switch at their own moment.

1
Zero-G RL experts
objects float until touched; gravity then switches on
2
Diverse demonstrations
reaching, grasping and pushing throughout 3D space
3
VLA distillation
fine-tune a VLA (π0.5) on the demonstrations
4
Earth-gravity tests
spatially harder versions of the same tasks

Compared against fixed and adaptive gravity randomization (OpenAI-style ADR) at the same cost. Next step: a physics curriculum that schedules the simulator’s physics (zero gravity, then reduced, then Earth gravity, then varied friction and mass) by how much each regime helps exploration, rather than widening one range around Earth values.

Prior work

Domain randomization varies physics as a robustness perturbation, around Earth gravity (OpenAI et al., 2019).

What would be new

Gravity as a controllable distribution shift that is used as the main training regime, not as a perturbation. Preliminary results: at heights only the zero-gravity experts trained on, they reach the object 2.4× and grasp or move it 2.4× as often as the best gravity-trained or gravity-randomized expert.

Idea 2

Metric language grounding for VLAs proposed

Train VLAs on commands with real units and 3D references, such as “move 2 inches left”, “lift it 3 cm, then carry it 5 cm right” or “go to the top-right front corner of the workspace”, to sharpen their spatial ability.

More from the simulator: The simulator knows exact positions, so metric language labels come for free from its state.

1
Workspace frame
a virtual 3D box around the workspace
2
Metric commands
distances, directions, angles and workspace-relative positions
3
Labels that match the motion
scripted moves in simulation, labeled from its state
4
Metric-supervised VLA
tested on ordinary, non-metric spatial tasks

Prior work

Language that describes the robot’s motion by direction only, auto-extracted from demonstrations (RT-H, LA4VLA); metric language as a navigation VLA’s output, such as “moving forward 75cm” (NaVILA); metric supervision of manipulation VLAs without language (Acting in Meters); metric spatial reasoning in vision-language models rather than action models (SpatialVLM, RoboTracer).

What would be new

To my knowledge, no manipulation VLA has yet been trained end to end to execute metric and workspace-relative commands; the real test is transfer to ordinary, non-metric spatial tasks. My pilot on existing expert rollouts found the labels exact (within the 1 cm rounding) but expert motion too fast and curved to label after the fact, so the data comes from scripted moves.

Idea 3

World-model minigames for agile skills early idea

Use learned world models to generate short, game-like tasks that demand speed and reaction (catching, intercepting a moving target, stabilizing a tumbling object) and let VLAs practice them. Such skills are richer than the slow, quasi-static tasks most robot data contains.

More from the simulator: World models make game content cheap: endless scenes and tasks from a prompt or an image, where a hand-built simulator needs every scene and asset authored.

1
World model
action-conditioned, learned from robot video
2
Minigames
catch, intercept, stabilize
3
Practice in imagination
fast, cheap rollouts for the VLA
4
Transfer
to simulation, then hardware

Prior work

Imitation policies trained entirely on demonstrations collected inside a learned world simulator, on tasks such as pushing, rope routing, grasping and sweeping (Interactive World Simulator, RSS 2026); controllable world models for policy evaluation and improvement (Ctrl-World); policy refinement inside diffusion world models (World4RL); synthetic robot trajectories from video world models (DreamGen). Generated, playable game worlds exist, but for 2D platformer games rather than robots (Genie).

What would be new

Agile skills are starting to get attention, as policies for moving targets (DynamicWAM) and as world models for state estimation in agile humanoid tasks (HAIC), but not as practice generated inside a world model. The open question is whether world models capture fast dynamics faithfully enough to train on: they already struggle to follow actions unlike their training data (Do Robotic World Models Really Follow Actions?).

Idea 4

Behavior-verified environment generation proposed

An agent that generates simulation environments together with executable behavioral tests, and red-teams each environment’s reward with cheap probe policies before any policy trains on it. Environments are kept only when they behave as intended.

More from the simulator: Generated environments become a trustworthy source of experience: each one comes with evidence that it behaves as intended and cannot be gamed.

From my own work: I designed the zero-gravity versions of eight ManiSkill3 tasks for my current project: containment walls that block the floating object but not the robot arm, and gravity that switches on at contact or grasp. The hardest bugs were behavioral, and none were visible in the scene itself: objects tunneling through thin walls, a physics material silently shared between objects and walls, a contact trigger firing on stale data right after a reset, and two reward exploits (a pose reward collectible while the object simply floated, and a lifting task counted as solved without the object ever being grasped). Each of these maps to an automatic test or a probe policy. Agent-generated environments will have the same kinds of bugs, at a scale no person can audit by hand.

1
Task in language
the environment to build
2
Environment and tests
scene, physics and executable behavioral checks
3
Red-team probes
zero, random, scripted and short-RL policies try to game the reward
4
Verified library
environments that pass, each with its evidence

Prior work

Agentic environment generation that checks the specification and layout of evaluation scenes (Isaac Lab-Arena, experimental, 2026); task and scene generation (RoboGen, GenSim), where people still check generated tasks by watching rendered videos; critics that review generated scenes and data for layout, silent failures and static physical plausibility (SceneSmith, V-CAGE, Code2Worlds-2); LLM-written reward code (Eureka).

What would be new

Checking how a generated environment behaves, not only how it looks: executable invariants plus reward red-teaming before training, for any simulator and any physics. Because the tests state the physics an environment is meant to have, unusual physics such as zero gravity is checked correctly instead of being flagged as a bug. A natural first project builds on the open-source Isaac Lab-Arena generator and measures how many hidden bugs and exploits the agent catches compared with a human audit, and whether policies trained on verified environments do better.

Idea 5

Imagination-guided experience generation early idea

A VLA imagines the outcome before it acts. Where the imagined and the simulated outcome disagree, it has found experience it cannot yet predict, and that gap decides which environments, tasks and physics the generator of idea 4 produces next. At deployment, the same gap signals that the world itself has changed, for example a different gravity, and triggers adaptation.

More from the simulator: Spend simulation on the experience the model cannot yet predict, instead of sampling scenes uniformly.

1
Imagine
predict the outcome of an action chunk
2
Act in simulation
observe what actually happens
3
Surprise
the gap between imagination and outcome
4
Generate more of it
environments, tasks and physics where the gap is large
↺ the improved model imagines again

Prior work

Imagining before acting with goal images or video (SuSIE, UniPi, GR-1); prediction error as curiosity for choosing actions (ICM, Plan2Explore); generating training environments by regret or novelty (PAIRED, POET); world models that flag task failures (Foresight).

What would be new

Using a VLA’s own imagination error to choose which environments to generate, at the level of tasks and physics rather than individual actions, so the simulator produces the experience the model most needs. The same signal doubles as a detector that the physics has changed at deployment, where world models have so far been used to flag task failures.

Space applications #

These directions build on my current work, which trains robots under zero gravity in simulation to make them better on Earth (Idea 1; project page). The same idea runs the other way. Robots for space are the extreme case of learning from simulation: real data from orbit or the Moon is almost nonexistent, so simulators with altered physics are where their experience has to come from, starting from models trained on Earth.

Idea 6

Adapting Earth-trained VLAs to microgravity planned

The reverse direction: take a VLA trained on Earth-gravity data and make it work when the physics changes, as in orbit.

More from the simulator: Reuse the same simulated tasks with new dynamics, to measure and train adaptation before any orbital hardware exists.

1
Earth-trained VLA
pretrained, terrestrial data
2
Microgravity tasks
same scenes, different dynamics
3
Adaptation
few-shot fine-tuning, dynamics-aware conditioning
4
Altered-gravity evaluation
measure what breaks and what transfers

Prior work

VLM and VLA work for space has so far been tested under Earth gravity: VLMs as operator agents, including OpenVLA fine-tuned on a lab robot arm to inspect satellite hardware (Carrasco et al., 2025); a lunar autonomy architecture whose VLA, adapted from 100 teleoperated demonstrations, succeeds on 29 of 30 in-domain trials on a physical lunar robot prototype, with only its traversal policy trained in simulated lunar gravity (SELENE, Tsinghua, 2026).

What would be new

A dynamics shift rather than a visual one, with VLA manipulation actually evaluated under altered gravity.

Idea 7

Metric object placement in microgravity proposed

Teach VLAs to move floating objects by metric, scene-relative commands, such as “put the tape 10 cm in front of the rack and let go” or “3 cm up and right, then 5 cm down”. On Earth a released object falls unless something holds it; in orbit it stays where it is put.

More from the simulator: In simulated microgravity any point in reach is a valid goal, and the outcome can be checked exactly.

1
Floating objects
a station workspace, gravity off throughout
2
Scene-relative commands
distances and directions relative to racks and surfaces
3
Goal-defined episodes
the command fixes the target point; labels match by construction
4
Exact placement check
the object rests within about 1 cm of the target

Prior work

Learned controllers for space robots so far handle free-flyer motion and docking rather than object placement (APIARY, Int-Ball2).

What would be new

A placement skill that only microgravity allows, with a precise, automatically checkable outcome, and the space counterpart of idea 2.

Idea 8

World models of the ISS and lunar workspaces proposed

Learn action-conditioned world models of real space workspaces, such as the interior of the International Space Station or lunar terrain, so VLAs can practice where hand-built simulators are hardest to make.

More from the simulator: Turn existing footage of space workspaces into an interactive practice environment, where building a faithful simulator by hand is the bottleneck.

1
Real space footage
ISS interior video, free-flyer cameras, lunar imagery
2
Inferred actions
inverse-dynamics or latent-action labels
3
Workspace world model
action-conditioned and visually faithful
4
VLA practice
a training ground for a generalist space VLA

Prior work

Space robot learning so far relies on hand-built simulators: an RL controller trained in NVIDIA Isaac Lab flew NASA’s Astrobee free-flyer, the first RL control of a free-flyer aboard the ISS (APIARY); RL docking for JAXA’s Int-Ball2 in an Isaac Sim model of an ISS module (Int-Ball2); photorealistic lunar simulation with a synthetic-data pipeline (OmniLRS); RL grasping in procedurally generated lunar scenes (Learning to Grasp on the Moon). World models have only just reached the space domain, for spacecraft docking with the ISS, trained on simulated flights (Out-of-this-World-Model, 2026). Video world models can label unlabeled video with inferred actions (DreamGen).

What would be new

To my knowledge, no world model yet covers the inside of a station or a lunar worksite for manipulation, or is learned from real footage. ISS footage is plentiful but carries no robot actions; a world model built from it would give a generalist space VLA visually realistic practice that no hand-built simulator offers.

Idea 9

A generalist VLA for space manipulation long-term vision

A pretrained, multi-task VLA for manipulation in space: a “π0.5 for space”.

More from the simulator: Build the space-manipulation dataset and benchmark from simulators with altered physics and lighting.

1
Space-manipulation dataset
simulation plus analog hardware
2
Benchmark
tasks × gravity × lighting
3
Multi-task pretraining
one model across tasks and conditions
4
Space operations
servicing, assembly, sampling

Prior work

Existing space work adapts models to one platform and a few tasks: OpenVLA fine-tuned on 10 episodes for lab inspection of satellite hardware (Carrasco et al., 2025); a VLA adapted from 100 demonstrations on a lunar robot prototype (SELENE, Tsinghua, 2026). Steps toward a space foundation model: a VLM adapted to extraterrestrial imagery, which reasons about images but does not output actions (Space-LLaVA, Stanford, 2024), and natural-language commands turned into constraint-satisfying spacecraft trajectories, also tested on a free-flying robot platform (SAGES, Stanford, 2025).

What would be new

To my knowledge no generalist exists yet. The bottleneck is data, so a space-manipulation dataset and benchmark is the most valuable first step, with the workspace world models of idea 8 as one source of that data.
